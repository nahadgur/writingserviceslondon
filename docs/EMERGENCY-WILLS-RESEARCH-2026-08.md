## Verified Legal Substance

Everything below is checked. Do not add anything to the page that is not.

**Section 9, Wills Act 1837.** A will must be in writing, signed by the testator or
by another person in their presence and at their direction, with the signature made
or acknowledged in the presence of two witnesses present at the same time, each of
whom then signs in the testator's presence. The "at their direction" route matters
when someone is too weak to hold a pen and is rarely explained.

**Section 15.** If a beneficiary, or the spouse or civil partner of a beneficiary,
witnesses the will, that gift fails. The will itself remains valid. In a hospital
room the available witnesses are usually the family, who are usually the
beneficiaries, so this is the practical trap.

**Video witnessing has expired.** The Wills Act 1837 (Electronic Communications)
(Amendment) (Coronavirus) Order 2020 permitted video witnessing from 31 January
2020 to 31 January 2024. The government confirmed it would not be renewed. From
1 February 2024 all wills in England and Wales must be witnessed by two people
physically present at the same time. Wills video witnessed inside that window
remain valid. Electronic signatures have never been valid for wills.

**The golden rule, Kenward v Adams (1975).** Where the testator is aged or has
suffered a serious illness, the will ought to be witnessed or approved by a medical
practitioner who satisfies themselves as to capacity and understanding, and who
records and preserves that examination. Best practice rather than law, but it is
the main protection against a later challenge. Google's People Also Ask for the
London head term includes "What is the golden rule in will writing?"

**Statutory wills.** Where the person has already lost capacity, a will can only be
made by application to the Court of Protection. GOV.UK operates an emergency
application route where the person has only a short time to live. This answers the
common question of what to do when a relative is dying and no longer has capacity.

**Privileged wills.** Section 11 Wills Act 1837, extended by the Wills (Soldiers
and Sailors) Act 1918, lets a soldier in actual military service, or a mariner or
seaman at sea, dispose of personal estate without the usual formalities, including
orally and without witnesses. Narrow, but it owns a query nobody targets.

## News Angle for 2025-26

The Law Commission published its final report, "Modernising Wills Law", on
16 May 2025. Volume II contains a draft Wills Bill. Key recommendations:

- A court dispensing power to validate informal wills that clearly reflect the
  testator's intentions. This is the single most relevant reform to emergency
  wills, because it is what would rescue a rushed deathbed will that fails the
  section 9 formalities.
- An enabling power allowing government to introduce electronic wills in future
  once safeguards exist, rather than immediately.
- Abolishing automatic revocation of a will on marriage.
- Lowering the minimum age for making a will from 18 to 16.

Status: awaiting a government decision on implementation. No incumbent page in
either SERP covers this.
